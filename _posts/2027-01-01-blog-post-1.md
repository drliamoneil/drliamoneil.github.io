To understand the meaning of the zero-order correlation, we must first learn the difference between a simple and partial correlation.

We quantify the amount of correlation between X and Y with a correlation coefficient that ranges from -1 to +1. The value of a coefficient represents the relative amount of correlation, and the sign of the coefficient represents the direction of the correlation. A perfect negative correlation by a coefficient of -1 and perfect positive correlation is a coefficient of +1. A total lack of correlation by a coefficient of 0. The most widely used correlation coefficient was formally presented in 1896 by Karl Pearson (Cowles, 1989). Pearson's correlation coefficient is symbolized by the letter _r_.

An issue with the simple correlation is that the correlation between X and Y could be due to a third variable, Z. X and Y being correlated because they are both related to Z. If this is the case, X and Y can be correlated even if there is no direct relationship between them. To understand the direct relationship between X and Y, we need to hold Z constant. This can be done experimentally, but it is often easier to use statistical control for Z by using it to predict X and Y. This allows us to extract the residuals of X and Y (i.e., the variance unaccounted for in X and Y by Z). The correlation of these residuals is the partial correlation (_pr_) between X and Y, having partialled out the variance of Z.

The order of a correlation indicates the number of variables that have been partialled out of the correlation. The more partialled the variables in the correlation the higher the order of the correlation. A zero-order correlation has no variables partialled out, first-order correlation has one variable partialled out, second-order correlation two variables partialled out, and so forth.
